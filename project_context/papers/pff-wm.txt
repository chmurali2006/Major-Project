ABSTRACT
Medical deepfakes and adversarial manipulations threaten AI-based diagnosis and telemedicine security. Existing 
watermarking methods for image authentication do not reliably distinguish clinically neutral transformations 
from semantic tampering, offer no interpretability of detected alterations, and often exceed latency constraints of 
clinical workflows. We propose Proactive Forensic Fragile Watermarking (PFF-WM), a framework that embeds 
two complementary watermarks: a fragile watermark in wavelet detail coefficients (pixel-level sensitivity) and a 
semi-robust watermark in the DCT domain whose embedding strength is modulated by a multi-scale attention 
map that prioritises diagnostically relevant regions. At the receiver side, a stacked autoencoder trained exclu-
sively on authentic images detects manipulations via reconstruction error, a lightweight refinement network 
produces a tamper localisation mask, and a gradient-based explainability layer estimates the clinical impact of 
any alteration. Experiments on CheXpert (resampled to 512 × 512), LiTS, and ISIC 2019 show that PFF-WM 
achieves 97.6% detection accuracy and an AUC of 0.989, with tamper localisation IoU of 83.2%, with a false 
positive rate below 0.5% under each tested non‑geometric benign transformation (JPEG, resizing, contrast, blur) 
and below 0.7% under chained non‑geometric transformations. Geometric transformations (rotation, translation) 
are a recognised limitation, reaching 18.7% FPR at 15◦ rotation.The method shows competitive or superior 
performance against existing watermarking‑based forensic methods under the tested conditions, although direct 
comparability is limited for methods designed for different resolutions or modalities. Inference time is 44 ms per 
512 × 512 image, making it computationally feasible for real‑time verification under the tested conditions.
1. Introduction
The integration of artificial intelligence into medical imaging has 
revolutionised computer‑aided diagnosis, telemedicine, and large‑scale 
image analysis (Hosny et al., 2025). However, this progress has also 
introduced critical security threats. Malicious actors can now generate 
highly realistic synthetic lesions (medical deepfakes), remove clinically 
significant findings, or craft imperceptible adversarial perturbations 
that deceive diagnostic models (Fikri et al., 2025). Unlike natural 
images, where tampering often targets entertainment or disinformation, 
manipulation of medical images directly endangers patient safety, 
compromises clinical decisions, and erodes trust in digital healthcare 
infrastructures (Sood et al., 2025). Throughout this paper, we distin-
guish between clinically neutral transformations and semantic 
tampering. A transformation is considered clinically neutral if it does not 
alter a board-certified radiologist's diagnostic interpretation. Examples 
include lossy compression (JPEG Q ≥ 70), resizing (90–110% of original 
dimensions), contrast adjustment (±10%), and mild Gaussian blur

a radiologist's diagnosis, including insertion of a synthetic lesion, 
removal of a real pathological finding, copy-move of a pathological re-
gion, or adversarial perturbation that flips a diagnostic classifier's output 
while preserving perceptual fidelity.Traditional image forensics operate 
passively detecting inconsistencies after an image has been transmitted 
and potentially altered (Khare et al., 2026). These methods are often 
brittle, require large annotated datasets of both pristine and manipu-
lated images, and struggle to generalise to novel attack types (Zhou 
et al., 2023). More importantly, they offer no proactive protection: the 
image is sent without any verifiable signal, and any tampering is 
detected only after the fact, potentially after a wrong diagnosis has 
already been made (Boukela et al., 2020). In high‑stakes medical envi-
ronments, a proactive approach is essential embedding a verifiable 
signal at the source that enables immediate and interpretable authen-
tication at the receiving end (Beggari et al., 2025). Digital watermarking 
has long been employed for copyright protection and integrity verifi-
cation (Yousefi Valandar et al., 2020). Recent research has extended 
watermarking to deepfake forensics, adversarial defence, and model 
protection (Sneha et al., 2020). Several proactive frameworks have been 
proposed that embed fragile or semi‑fragile watermarks to detect ma-
nipulations. Other works focus on robustness to common image pro-
cessing or on training‑free operation (Mathivanan & Ganesh, 2021). 
Despite these advances, existing methods share critical limitations when 
applied to medical imaging. They typically treat all pixel alterations 
equally, failing to distinguish benign transformations (lossy compres-
sion, resizing) from semantically critical manipulations (Chattopadhyay 
et al., 2020). Moreover, they output only a binary authenticity decision 
or a coarse tamper map, without any explanation of how a detected 
manipulation would affect a specific clinical diagnosis (Borra & Thanki, 
2019). Many approaches are computationally heavy, require low‑reso-
lution inputs, or need retraining for each new watermark making them 
impractical for real‑time clinical workflows (Euschi et al., 2026). To 
address these gaps, we identify three fundamental challenges that any 
practical medical image watermarking system must overcome. First, the 
watermark must be fragile to semantic alterations (such as insertion or 
deletion of pathological findings) yet robust to routine medical image 
processing (compression, resizing, contrast adjustment) (Khafaga et al., 
2023). Second, the system must localise manipulated regions with pix-
el‑level precision and simultaneously provide an interpretable assess-
ment of the clinical impact (Thind et al., 2026). Third, the solution must 
be computationally efficient, support high‑resolution medical images, 
and avoid retraining for each new watermark payload (Anand & Singh, 
2023). In this paper, we propose Proactive Forensic Fragile Water-
marking (PFF‑WM), a novel framework designed to meet these chal-
lenges. Our approach embeds two complementary watermarks: a fragile 
watermark inserted into wavelet detail coefficients for pixel‑level 
sensitivity, and a semi‑robust watermark embedded into mid‑frequency 
DCT coefficients whose embedding strength is guided by a multi‑scale 
attention map that highlights diagnostically relevant regions. At the 
receiver side, a stacked autoencoder trained exclusively on authentic 
watermarked images detects manipulations by analysing reconstruction 
errors. A lightweight refinement network transforms the error map into 
a precise tamper localisation mask. Finally, an explainability layer uses 
gradient backpropagation through a frozen medical classifier to quantify 
the clinical impact of the detected alteration. The entire system is 
trainable end‑to‑end and operates natively at high resolution without 
requiring retraining for new watermarks. The remainder of this paper is 
organised as follows. Section 2 reviews related work and identifies gaps 
specific to medical image authentication. Section 3 describes the 
PFF-WM framework. Section 4 presents experimental setup, results, and 
ablation studies. Section 5 concludes with a discussion of limitations and 
future work.
2. Related work
Digital watermarking has evolved from traditional copyright pro-
tection to address emerging challenges in deepfake forensics, adversarial 
machine learning, model intellectual property protection, and 
cyber‑physical system security (Awasthi et al., 2026). Recent advances 
leverage deep learning, optimization algorithms, and conventional 
transform‑domain techniques to enable proactive content authentica-
tion, tamper localisation, and attack detection (Zhou et al., 2024). This 
section organises the literature into four thematic areas: proactive 
deepfake detection and authentication, adversarial watermarking for 
model protection and image attack, secure and robust image water-
marking with deep learning, and watermarking for cyber‑physical sys-
tems security.
2.1. Proactive deepfake detection and authentication
The proliferation of deepfake technology has motivated proactive 
forensic methods that embed verifiable signals into images before 
dissemination (Farri & Ayubi, 2018). Unlike passive detectors that 
analyse post‑manipulation artifacts, proactive approaches enable 
tamper detection and source tracing at verification time (Anand & 
Singh, 2020). Abrar and Sheikh (Abrar & Sheikh, 2026) propose Deep-
Mark, a semi‑fragile framework that embeds multi‑bit payloads into 
images and videos using a U‑Net encoder‑decoder. A curriculum 
learning strategy progressively exposes the model to benign distortions 
followed by malicious manipulations, and tamper localisation is ach-
ieved via thresholded difference maps. Zhao et al. (Zhao et al., 2024) 
introduce a proactive manipulation detection method that hides an 
entire secret image as an invisible watermark using a cover‑agnostic 
U‑Net. An attack module with distortion and tampering layers enforces 
semi‑fragility, and tampered regions are identified by comparing the 
recovered secret image with the original. Wu et al. (Wu et al., 2026) 
present ConMark, a conditional watermarking framework that injects 
condition vectors via Feature‑wise Linear Modulation (FiLM). A light-
weight condition network allows three variants: conditioned decoder for 
flexible robustness, conditioned encoder to improve downstream de-
tector accuracy, and joint conditioning for simultaneous provenance 
tracking, detection, and detectability enhancement. Yuan et al. (Yuan 
et al., 2026) propose Adversarial Semi‑Fragile Watermarking (ASFW), 
a dual‑branch defence that combines adversarial perturbations (to 
disrupt deepfake models) with semi‑fragile watermarking (to enable 
post‑hoc authentication). A differentiable JPEG compression module is 
integrated during training to ensure robustness under lossy trans-
mission. Lai et al. (Lai et al., 2026) develop TFPDD, a training‑free 
proactive detection scheme that synergises conventional robust water-
marking with identity‑aware hashing. The core innovation is Banded 
and Interleaved Quantization Index Modulation (BI‑QIM), which uses a 
hexagonal lattice to create directional decoding regions, significantly 
enhancing robustness against common signal processing. A blind syn-
chronization mechanism embeds least significant bits of the face 
bounding box coordinates, enabling precise face reconstruction without 
shared reference masks. A comprehensive survey by Lai et al. (Lai et al., 
2025) reviews proactive forensic techniques for deepfake based on 
digital watermarking, classifying them into robust, semi‑fragile, and 
dual watermarking strategies, and discusses open challenges such as the 
lack of standardised benchmarks.

Adversarial Visible Watermarking (RAVW), which uses Grad‑CAM to 
identify important regions for watermark embedding. A generator

(Fei et al., 2025) present a distributor‑centric model watermarking 
method for generative models using Watermark‑Informed Convolution 
(WIC). Standard convolution layers are replaced with multiple parallel 
kernels, and a watermark encoder modulates these kernels at both 
kernel level and filter level. Once fine‑tuned, a new watermark can be 
embedded in milliseconds by pre‑computing the combined kernel, with 
no inference overhead and robustness to model pruning or image 
compression.
2.3. Other watermarking applications
Beyond multimedia forensics and adversarial attacks, watermarking 
has been applied to secure image transmission and cyber‑physical sys-
tems. Thind et al. (Hamami et al., 2026) propose SECURENet, an 
encoder‑decoder architecture that embeds two secret images into a 
single cover image. A preparation network processes each watermark, a 
hiding network concatenates features with the cover image, and a reveal 
network extracts the watermarks. A noise layer simulates various attacks 
(Gaussian noise, blurring, geometric distortions, JPEG compression) 
during training to enhance robustness. Chaotic encryption with a 
128‑bit key scrambles the watermarked image before transmission, 
ensuring that only authorised users can recover the watermarks. Liu 
et al. (Liu et al., 2023) propose a hybrid proactive detection scheme for 
cyber‑physical systems that combines additive watermarking and 
moving target defence for systems with unknown‑but‑bounded distur-
bances. A zonotopic observer estimates system states, and the detection 
residual is monitored against a zonotope‑based threshold. Watermarks 
(pseudo‑random sequences) are added to both control input and mea-
surement output channels, while a time‑varying linear transformation 
acts as a moving target. Both are removed at the receiver side when no 
attack is present, ensuring zero performance degradation. The method is 
effective against denial‑of‑service, replay, and covert attacks, provided 
the moving target parameters remain unknown to the adversary.
2.4. Research gap and motivation
The literature reviewed above shows progress in watermarking for 
deepfake forensics, but four gaps persist for medical imaging. First, most 
proactive methods output a binary decision or a coarse tamper map 
without explaining how a manipulation would affect a clinical diag-
nosis. Second, adversarial watermarking techniques (RAVW, MIS-
PSO‑Attack, WIC) are designed to mislead classifiers or protect model 
IP, not to detect semantically meaningful medical manipulations such as 
lesion insertion or removal. Third, dual‑watermark strategies (ConMark, 
ASFW) combine robust and fragile watermarks but either increase 
model complexity or require retraining per watermark; none integrate 
an explicit explainability layer linking watermark distortion to clinical 
impact. Fourth, deep learning‑based frameworks like SECURENet 
require low‑resolution inputs and extensive training, limiting their use 
in high‑resolution medical workflows. Table 1 summarises the key 
characteristics, advantages, and limitations of the reviewed water-
marking methods across the four domains. Most existing proactive 
deepfake detection methods output only a binary authenticity decision 
or a coarse tamper map. While approaches like DeepMark and the 
scheme of Zhao et al. can localise manipulated regions at the pixel level, 
they do not explain why a region is considered tampered or how the 
alteration would affect a clinical diagnosis. This lack of interpretability 
severely limits trust and adoption in medical environments, where un-
derstanding the clinical impact of a manipulation is as important as 
detecting it. Current adversarial watermarking techniques (RAVW, 
MISPSO‑Attack, WIC, and others) are primarily designed to mislead 
classifiers or protect model intellectual property. They are not optimised 
for detecting subtle, semantically meaningful manipulations such as the 
insertion or removal of pathological lesions – a common form of medical 
deepfake. Moreover, these methods treat all modifications equally, 
failing to distinguish between benign transformations (e.g., lossy 
compression, resizing) and malicious tampering that alters diagnostic 
content.
Dual‑watermark strategies proposed in works like ConMark and 
ASFW combine robust and fragile watermarks, but they either increase 
model complexity or require retraining for each new watermark. None 
of them integrate an explicit explainability layer that links watermark 
distortion to clinical impact. Furthermore, existing approaches rarely 
exploit multi‑scale attention mechanisms to guide watermark embed-
ding into diagnostically relevant regions, leaving critical areas vulner-
able to undetected manipulation. Finally, deep learning‑based 
frameworks such as SECURENet achieve excellent visual quality and 
robustness but are computationally heavy and demand large training 
datasets. Their application to medical imaging is further hindered by the 
need for high‑resolution inputs and the scarcity of annotated medical 
manipulation data. Orthogonal moment watermarking embeds water-
marks in fractional-order quaternion exponent moments, where water-
mark integrity is compromised only under semantic deepfake attacks 
(Wang et al., 2026). A residual diffusion model attack treats watermark 
information as noise and reconstructs the original host image through a 
Markov chain on residuals, achieving strong watermark removal with 
high visual quality (Wang et al., 2026). Given these limitations, several 
challenges must be addressed to enable practical and trustworthy 
watermarking for medical image authentication. A first challenge is to 
design a watermark that remains fragile to critical semantic alterations 
(such as lesion insertion or removal) while staying robust to routine 
medical image processing (compression, resizing, contrast adjustment). 
A second challenge is to localise manipulated regions with pixel‑level 
precision and, at the same time, provide an interpretable explanation of 
how the alteration would affect a clinical diagnosis. A third challenge is 
to develop a dual‑watermark strategy that separates fine‑grained 
tamper detection from a semi‑robust reference, without incurring 
excessive computational cost or requiring retraining for each new 
watermark. The ultimate goal is to propose an approach that bridges 
conventional watermarking and modern AI forensics, offering a solution 
capable of detecting, localising, and explaining manipulations in medi-
cal images, thereby supporting secure AI‑assisted diagnosis, telemedi-
cine, and medical data sharing.

(PFF‑WM) framework, dedicated entirely to the description of the 
watermark generation, integration (embedding) and extraction (detec-
tion) processes. Let a medical image be denoted by I∈ RH×W×C with 
height H, width W and number of channels C (typically C= 1 for X‑rays 
or CT slices, C= 3 for RGB histopathology or dermatology images). A 
watermarking system comprises an embedding function E : RH×W×C×

distinction by encoding diagnostic saliency directly into the water-

The PFF‑WM architecture comprises five sequential modules that 
operate in two phases: embedding (performed once at the point of 
acquisition) and detection (performed at each verification). The first 
module is a multi‑scale attention module that computes a diagnostic 
saliency map. The second is the dual watermark embedding module, 
which simultaneously injects a fragile watermark and a semi‑robust 
reference watermark into distinct transform‑domain coefficients. The 
third is a stacked autoencoder detector that detects manipulations by 
analysing the reconstruction error of the extracted watermarks. The 
fourth is an attention‑based localisation module that produces a pix-
el‑precise tamper map. The fifth is an explainability layer that generates 
a clinical impact assessment via gradient backpropagation through a 
frozen medical classifier. The forward pass proceeds as follows. The 
input image I first passes through the attention module to obtain sa-
liency weights. These weights guide the embedding module, which in-
jects the two watermarks. The watermarked image Iw is then transmitted 
over an untrusted channel. At the receiver side, the detection module 
extracts both watermarks, computes reconstruction errors, localises 
discrepancies and produces an explainability report. All modules except 
the frozen classifier are trainable end‑to‑end. This separation of 
embedding from detection is motivated by two practical considerations: 
embedding can be computationally intensive and is performed only once 
at acquisition, while detection must be lightweight and repeated many 
times; moreover, the dual‑watermark strategy requires independent 
optimisation objectives for fragility and semi‑robustness, which are 
more effectively balanced through separate loss terms than through a 
single auto‑encoder.

3.2. Dual watermark embedding strategy
The core innovation lies in embedding two complementary water-
marks that operate in different transform domains with distinct sensi-
tivity profiles. Let T denote a transformation from the spatial domain to 
a coefficient domain. We use the Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT) 
with the Haar basis for the fragile watermark and the Discrete Cosine 
Transform (DCT) for the semi‑robust watermark. This hybrid choice is 
justified: DWT provides spatial‑frequency localisation that enables 
precise tamper localisation, while DCT coefficients exhibit predictable 
degradation under JPEG compression, making them suitable for a 
semi‑robust reference. The embedding process starts from the cover 
image I (Thanki et al., 2017): 
CDWT = DWT(I)={LL, LH, HL, HH}, CDCT = DCT(I). (5) 
Here LL is the approximation subband (low‑low frequencies), LH,
HL, HH are the detail subbands (horizontal, vertical and diagonal high 
frequencies), and CDCT is the DCT coefficient matrix.

3.2.4. Embedding capacity analysis
The fragile watermark has capacity N_f equal to the number of DWT 
detail coefficients (LH, HL, HH) per spatial location. For a 512 × 512 
grayscale image with 3-level DWT decomposition, the total number of 
detail coefficients is approximately 3 * (256^2 + 128^2 + 64^2) = 3 * 
(65,536 + 16,384 + 4096) = 3 * 86,016 = 258,048 bits. We use 
quantisation step delta_f = 8, which embeds one bit per coefficient, 
yielding a maximum fragile payload of 258 kbits for the full image. The 
semi-robust watermark has capacity N_sr equal to the number of mid- 
frequency DCT coefficients selected by the band T_low = 0.15*min (H, 
W) and T_high = 0.35*min (H, W). For 512 × 512, the DCT is applied in 
8 × 8 blocks, producing 4096 blocks each with 64 coefficients. The mid- 
frequency band per block contains approximately 20–25 coefficients 
depending on the exact threshold, giving N_sr ≈ 4096 * 22 ≈ 90 kbits. In 
practice, we use a smaller payload (N_f = 64 coefficients per spatial 
location, N_sr = 32 coefficients per spatial location) to reduce compu-
tational overhead while maintaining detection performance. In our ex-
periments, we use a practical subset (Nf = 64 coefficients per location, 
Nsr = 32 coefficients per location) to reduce computational overhead 
while maintaining detection performance.

4. Experiments and results
This section evaluates the proposed Proactive Forensic Fragile 
Watermarking (PFF-WM) framework on multiple medical imaging 
datasets, comparing it against state-of-the-art methods from the litera-
ture. We assess detection accuracy, tamper localisation precision, 
robustness to benign transformations, explainability quality, and 
computational efficiency. Ablation studies isolate the contribution of 
each architectural component.
4.1. Experimental setup
4.1.1. Datasets
We use three public medical image datasets spanning different mo-
dalities and diagnostic tasks: CheXpert (5000 frontal chest X-rays, 224 ×
224 resolution) for pneumonia and cardiomegaly detection (Irvin et al., 
2019); LiTS (Liver Tumour Segmentation, 130 CT volumes, 512 × 512 
slices) for liver lesion insertion/removal (Bilic et al., 2023); and ISIC 
2019 (2500 dermoscopy images, 512 × 512) for melanoma detection 
(Codella et al., 2019). For CheXpert (native resolution 224 × 224), all 
images were upsampled to 512 × 512 using bicubic interpolation to 
maintain a consistent embedding and detection pipeline across datasets. 
Runtime measurements were performed exclusively at 512 × 512. 
Metrics are therefore comparable across datasets. Each dataset is split 
into training (60%), validation (20%), and test (20%). Only the training 
set is used for watermark embedding model training (autoencoder and 
attention modules). The validation set is used for threshold selection, 
and the test set for final evaluation.
4.1.2. Manipulation generation
To evaluate detection and localisation, we generate four types of 
manipulations on test images: adversarial attacks (FGSM with ϵ = 4 and 
PGD with 3 steps, ϵ= 4, step=1) targeting a pretrained DenseNet-121 
classifier; medical deepfakes (GAN-based lesion insertion using Style-
GAN2 trained on lesion patches, and diffusion-based lesion removal 
using DDPM inpainting); classic tampering (copy-move of random 32 ×
32 patches and removal via inpainting with Gaussian blur); and benign 
transformations (JPEG compression at quality 70, 85, 95, resizing to 
90% of original size, contrast adjustment of ±10%, Gaussian blur with σ 
= 0.5). For each test image, we apply exactly one manipulation type, 
except for robustness tests where multiple benign transformations are 
chained. Ground-truth manipulation masks are available for localisation 
evaluation.
4.1.3. Evaluation metrics
Detection performance is measured using accuracy, precision, recall, 
F1-score, and area under the ROC curve (AUC). Localisation accuracy is 
quantified by the intersection over union (IoU) between the predicted 
tamper mask and the ground truth, pixel accuracy, and pixel-wise F1- 
score (Sayah et al., 2024). Robustness is assessed as the detection rate 
(or false positive rate) under benign transformations, with the design 
target of maintaining a false positive rate below 1% while the fragile 
watermark degrades as expected. Explainability is evaluated via (i) the 
Pearson correlation between the predicted tamper mask M and the 
Grad‑CAM attention map of a frozen medical classifier (DenseNet‑121) 
(Dwivedi et al., 2026), and (ii) human evaluation by two board‑certified 
radiologists. For the human evaluation, 100 manipulated images were 
randomly selected from the CheXpert test set (50 with GAN‑based lesion 
insertion, 50 with diffusion‑based removal). Both radiologists were 
blinded to the ground truth, to the manipulation type, and to each 
other's ratings. They independently rated the clinical relevance of the 
predicted tamper mask on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = "no clinical rele-
vance", 5 = "critical clinical relevance") using a standardised rubric: 1 =
region has no anatomical structure; 2 = region corresponds to 
non-diagnostic anatomy; 3 = region corresponds to diagnostic anatomy 
but alteration is minor; 4 = region corresponds to diagnostic anatomy 
and alteration would affect assessment; 5 = region corresponds to a 
critical finding and alteration would change diagnosis. Inter-rater 
agreement was measured using Cohen's kappa, yielding kappa = 0.81 
(substantial agreement). Reported scores are the average of the two 
raters. Imperceptibility of the embedded watermark is measured using 
peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) and the structural similarity index 
(SSIM).

1 × 10  3 for the stacked autoencoder detector, as the autoencoder

single NVIDIA A100 GPU (40 GB memory). Training proceeds in three 
phases to avoid instability from multi-objective optimisation: Phase 1 
(embedding pretraining, 10 epochs) minimises only L fragile to learn a 
clean watermark embedding; Phase 2 (joint embedding-detector 
training, 30 epochs) adds L detection and trains both parts simulta-

4.2. Detection performance
Table 2 reports the detection performance on the CheXpert test set 
for all manipulation types. PFF-WM achieves the highest overall accu-
racy (97.6%) and AUC (0.989), significantly outperforming DeepMark 
(Abrar & Sheikh, 2026) (94.1%) and ASFW (Yuan et al., 2026) (95.2%).
The improvement is most pronounced for medical deepfakes (lesion 
insertion/removal), where PFF-WM reaches 96.8% accuracy versus 
88.3% for DeepMark (Abrar & Sheikh, 2026). This confirms hypothesis 
H1: attention-guided dual watermarking is particularly effective against 
semantic manipulations that alter diagnostic content. Fig. 4 presents the 
ROC curves, where PFF-WM maintains a true positive rate above 0.95 at 
a false positive rate of 0.01.
When breaking down the performance of PFF-WM by manipulation 
type, the method achieves 98.2% accuracy on adversarial attacks 
(FGSM/PGD), 96.5% on GAN-based lesion insertion, 97.1% on 
diffusion-based lesion removal, 98.9% on copy-move tampering, and 
97.8% on removal by inpainting. On benign transformations (where the 
image is expected to be classified as authentic), the true negative rate is 
99.3%. The slightly lower performance on GAN-based lesion insertion is 
due to the high visual quality of generated lesions that occasionally 
mimic natural textures; nevertheless, the fragile watermark still de-
grades sufficiently for detection.
Table 3 compares PFF-WM performance on localised versus diffuse 
pathologies. For diffuse interstitial lung disease, detection accuracy 
drops by 6.9 percentage points and AUC falls from 0.992 to 0.912 
compared to localised pneumonia or nodules. The underlying cause is 
attention sparsity: the multi-scale attention module (Section 3.1) pro-
duces a saliency map that is widely dispersed when pathology is 
distributed throughout the lung parenchyma rather than concentrated in 
a focal region. With attention sparsity exceeding 40%, the embedding 
strength beta(i,j) = beta_0 * (1 + lambda_attn * A(i,j)) becomes nearly 
uniform across the image. Consequently, the semi-robust watermark 
loses its diagnostic prioritisation, making it less discriminative for se-
mantic manipulations in low-saliency areas. The fragile watermark re-
mains intact and continues to detect pixel-level changes, but the dual- 
watermark decision rule becomes less selective. This represents a 
known limitation of the current attention mechanism.

4.4. Robustness to benign transformations
A key requirement is that the semi-robust watermark survives 
routine medical image processing, while the fragile watermark degrades 
(but detection should not falsely trigger on authentic images). Table 8
reports the false positive rate on authentic watermarked images sub-
jected to benign transformations. PFF‑WM maintains a false positive 
rate below 0.5% under each tested non‑geometric individual benign 
transformation (JPEG, resizing, contrast, blur) and below 0.7% under 
chained non‑geometric benign transformations. The following results 
exclude geometric transformations (rotation, translation), which are 
analysed separately in Table 9.
The low false positive rate is due to the dual-watermark decision 
rule: only when both δf and δsr exceed thresholds does the system clas-
sify as manipulated. Benign transformations raise δf but leave δsr low, so 
the image is correctly deemed authentic. Table 9 evaluates PFF-WM 
under geometric transformations that were not included in the orig-
inal robustness analysis. Small rotations (5 degrees) and translations (5 
pixels) are detected with high accuracy (above 94%), but the false 
positive rate increases with transformation magnitude, reaching 18.7% 
for 15-degree rotation. This degradation occurs because both the fragile 
watermark (embedded in DWT detail coefficients) and the semi-robust 
watermark (embedded in DCT mid-frequencies) are sensitive to spatial 
misalignment. Unlike JPEG compression or contrast adjustment, which 
preserve pixel locations, geometric transformations disrupt the 
synchronisation between embedding and extraction coordinates. The 
restoration step (resizing back to original dimensions for scaling) 
partially mitigates the effect, but rotation and translation remain 
challenging.
Table 10 confirms that the orthogonality assumption underlying Eq. 
(12) holds empirically across all three datasets. The mean absolute 
correlation between host mid-frequency DCT coefficients and the 
spreading sequence p is below 0.01, and the maximum correlation 
observed over 5000 test images is 0.021, which is an order of magnitude 
smaller than the embedded watermark energy (beta_0 = 0.1). This 
validates that the despreading operation reliably recovers the semi- 
robust watermark without interference from the host signal.
4.5. Explainability evaluation
Hypothesis H3 states that the explainability layer produces clinically 
interpretable outputs. We evaluate this in two ways: (i) the Pearson 
correlation between the predicted tamper mask M and the Grad-CAM 
attention of the frozen DenseNet-121 classifier, and (ii) human evalua-
tion by two board-certified radiologists. Table 11 shows that the full 
PFF-WM with the alignment loss achieves a correlation of 0.78 and a 
radiologist score of 4.3 out of 5, both substantially higher than using 
only the raw difference map from DeepMark (Abrar & Sheikh, 2026) or 
the adversarial map from ASFW (Yuan et al., 2026). The 
Grad-CAM-based explanation described in Section 3 relies on a frozen 
classifier, DenseNet-121, fine-tuned on the target dataset. For rare dis-
eases or unannotated datasets where such a classifier is not available, we 
investigated two alternative conFig. urations. First, we replaced the 
fine-tuned classifier with a DenseNet-121 pretrained only on ImageNet 
(no medical fine-tuning). The Pearson correlation between the predicted 
tamper mask and the resulting Grad-CAM maps dropped from 0.78 to 
0.43, and the radiologist score fell from 4.3 to 2.2 (out of 5). Second, we 
replaced the classifier entirely with a generic feature extractor 
(ResNet-50 trained on ImageNet) and computed sensitivity as the 
Euclidean distance between feature maps of the original and manipu-
lated images. This unsupervised alternative achieved a Pearson corre-
lation of 0.38 and a radiologist score of 2.0. These results indicate that 
task-specific medical knowledge is essential for clinically meaningful 
explanations. Consequently, PFF-WM as currently formulated is not 
recommended for pathologies without a reliable pre-trained classifier. 
The alignment loss (L explain) alone improves the correlation from 0.52 
to 0.78, confirming its importance for clinical interpretability.

We conduct ablation experiments on CheXpert to quantify the 
contribution of each component: removing attention-guided embedding 
(uniform β), removing the dual-watermark (using only the fragile 
watermark), removing the stacked autoencoder (direct thresholding on 
δf and δsr), removing the alignment loss, and replacing the multi-scale 
attention with a single 5 × 5 kernel. Table 12 summarises the results.
The dual-watermark strategy and the stacked autoencoder are the 
most critical for detection performance, while attention guidance im-
proves localisation IoU by 3.1 points, confirming that concentrating 
embedding in diagnostically relevant regions aids precise tamper 
mapping.

4.8. Discussion and future work
The experimental results provide strong support for the three hy-
potheses that guided this work. Regarding H1, the measured fragility 
ratio, defined as the increase in semi‑robust watermark error under se-
mantic manipulations relative to benign transformations, reaches 
approximately 6.2. This large separation confirms that attention‑guided 
embedding successfully concentrates watermark energy in diagnosti-
cally critical regions, making the system selectively sensitive to clini-
cally meaningful alterations. For H2, the stacked autoencoder detector 
achieves a detection AUC of 0.989, substantially higher than the 0.963 
obtained by direct thresholding of the raw watermark errors, thereby 
validating that reconstruction‑based anomaly detection on the 
dual‑watermark feature tensor provides a more powerful and invariant 
statistic for manipulation detection. H3 is supported by the Pearson 
correlation of 0.78 between the predicted tamper mask and the 
Grad‑CAM attention of a frozen medical classifier, as well as by the 
average radiologist score of 4.3 out of 5, indicating that the alignment 
loss effectively forces the localisation module to highlight regions that 
are clinically relevant. When compared to existing proactive forensic 
methods, PFF‑WM shows clear advantages. DeepMark and ASFW, both 
designed primarily for natural images, lack any mechanism to incor-
porate diagnostic saliency, which explains their lower performance on 
medical deepfakes. SECURENet, while robust, operates on heavily 
downsampled images (64 × 64) and thus loses the fine spatial details 
essential for lesion detection in high‑resolution medical data. TFPDD, 
although fast and training‑free, relies on detectable facial features and 
therefore cannot be applied to most medical imaging modalities. The 
proposed framework overcomes these limitations by combining 
domain‑specific attention with a dual‑watermark strategy and an 
explainable detection pipeline. To resolve any remaining ambiguity, the 
theoretical false positive rate of 10⁻⁴ (0.01%) is a design goal used for 
threshold selection on a clean validation set of authentic watermarked 
images with no post-embedding processing. The empirical rate of 0.7% 
reported in Table 8 is a test-set measurement under chained benign 
transformations (JPEG + resizing + contrast). These two Figures are not 
contradictory because the latter includes transformation-induced 
watermark degradation that the former explicitly excludes. In Section 
3.2.3, we describe two conceptually different criteria. The 10⁻⁴ theo-
retical false positive rate assumes (i) no benign transformations, (ii) 
independence of δf and δsr, and (iii) an ideal infinite sample. It is used 
only as a design goal for choosing the quantisation step Δf. The actual 
thresholds τf and τsr are selected empirically via grid search on the 
validation set to balance a detection rate >99% on manipulated images 
against a low false positive rate on authentic images. This is standard 
practice in fragile watermarking and avoids over‑optimistic theoretical 
claims.
4.8.1. Limitation of the explainability module for rare diseases
The Grad-CAM-based explanation requires a pre-trained medical 
classifier fine-tuned on the target pathology. For rare diseases or un-
annotated datasets where such a classifier is unavailable, the explain-
ability module cannot operate in its current form. Replacing the fine- 
tuned DenseNet-121 with an ImageNet-pretrained version reduces the 
Pearson correlation from 0.78 to 0.43, while a generic ResNet-50 feature 
extractor further drops the correlation to 0.38. These results indicate 
that domain-specific medical knowledge is essential for clinically 
meaningful explanations. Consequently, we do not recommend using 
the explainability module of PFF-WM for pathologies without a reliable 
pre-trained classifier. This is not a minor limitation but a fundamental 
constraint of the current design: the Grad-CAM-based explanation 
cannot be replaced by a generic feature extractor without losing clinical 
meaning (Pearson correlation drops from 0.78 to 0.38). However, the 
detection and localisation modules remain fully operational. For future 
work, foundation models such as Med-SAM or BioViL-T could provide a 
promising alternative: these models generate dense feature embeddings 
without task-specific training, and a perturbation-based sensitivity 
analysis could estimate clinical impact by measuring feature distortion 
after masking the tampered region. The comparative evaluation in 
Section 4.2 includes methods (DeepMark, ASFW, ConMark, SECURENet, 
TFPDD) that were not all designed for medical imaging or for the same 
operating conditions. SECURENet operates on heavily downsampled 64 
× 64 images and therefore loses fine spatial details critical for lesion 
detection; its lower performance on 512 × 512 medical images is ex-
pected. TFPDD relies on detectable facial features and cannot be applied 
to chest X-rays or CT slices at all; we included it only to demonstrate that 
training-free methods are not a viable alternative for medical modalities 
without facial structure. DeepMark and ASFW, while designed for im-
ages, do not incorporate diagnostic saliency or explainability. The per-
formance gap reported should therefore be interpreted as evidence that 
domain-specific design choices (attention guidance, dual-watermark, 
explainability) confer advantages for medical deepfake detection, not 
as a claim that PFF-WM universally outperforms these methods on their 
original tasks. A fairer comparison would require re-engineering these 
baselines for medical imaging, which is beyond the scope of this work.
4.8.2. Performance on diffuse pathologies
Diffusely distributed pathologies such as interstitial lung disease or 
pulmonary fibrosis reduce the effectiveness of the attention guidance 
mechanism. When the diagnostic finding spans large areas of the image, 
the attention map A(i,j) becomes widely dispersed (attention sparsity >
40%), causing the semi-robust watermark's embedding strength to 
approach a constant value. This reduces the fragility ratio rho from 
approximately 6.2 for localised lesions to approximately 2.8 for diffuse 
pathologies. PFF-WM still detects manipulations in diffuse pathologies, 
but with lower confidence (AUC 0.912 vs 0.992). This degradation is 
inherent to any attention-based mechanism that assumes pathology has 
a focal spatial signature. Future work could address this limitation by 
replacing the single attention map with a multi-branch architecture that 
includes a "global context" branch explicitly designed for diffuse pat-
terns, or by using an adaptive temperature parameter tau in the softmax 
normalisation (Eq. (4)) that is learned per image based on the entropy of 
the raw attention map.
4.8.3. Generalisation to unseen deepfake attacks
PFF-WM generalises reasonably well to unseen deepfake generators, 
maintaining an AUC above 0.93 without retraining. However, a 
completely novel attack distribution that differs substantially from the 
training set in terms of perturbation statistics (e.g., amplitude spectrum, 
spatial localisation, or frequency content) could reduce detection ac-
curacy further. The framework does not require retraining for each new 
watermark payload, thanks to the key-dependent pseudo-random gen-
eration of both watermarks. Nevertheless, to handle evolving deepfake 
threats without manual intervention, future work will incorporate an 
adaptive attack simulation module that dynamically generates new 
manipulation strategies based on the current detector's failure modes.

This could be implemented as a generative adversarial training loop 
where a generator learns to produce manipulations that maximise the 
autoencoder's reconstruction error on authentic images, while the de-
tector adapts online. The manipulations evaluated in this work (FGSM/ 
PGD, StyleGAN2 lesion insertion, DDPM removal, copy-move, inpaint-
ing) represent a broad range of tampering strategies, but they are syn-
thetically generated and may not fully replicate real-world medical 
deepfakes produced by adversaries with access to clinical data. Our 
threat model assumes that the attacker can modify the watermarked 
image after transmission but does not have access to the secret key k, the 
attention map, or the autoencoder's parameters. Under this model, the 
attacker's goal is to alter the diagnostic content (e.g., remove a tumour) 
while preserving perceptual quality. The unseen attack evaluation in 
Table 5 (Med-DDIM, StarGAN-v2) provides a stronger test of general-
isation than evaluating only seen attacks. However, we acknowledge 
that a definitive assessment of clinical realism would require a pro-
spective study with real tampering attempts by radiologists or adver-
sarial AI systems, which we leave to future work.
4.8.4. Sensitivity to geometric transformations
Geometric transformations such as rotation and translation pose a 
challenge for PFF-WM. The method is sensitive beyond small magni-
tudes, with a false positive rate of 18.7% at 15-degree rotation. This is 
due to spatial synchronisation loss in DWT and DCT domains. Future 
work could employ Fourier-Mellin transform or attention-based dy-
namic realignment to achieve geometric invariance. We acknowledge 
that this sensitivity is not a minor limitation but a genuine security 
vulnerability. Attackers could deliberately rotate or translate images to 
evade detection. Therefore, PFF‑WM should not be relied upon in sce-
narios where geometric attacks are likely unless explicitly paired with a 
synchronisation pre‑processing step. Because routine clinical workflows 
may involve lossless rotation, cropping, or registration before interpre-
tation, PFF‑WM in its current form must be preceded by a synchroni-
sation module that re‑aligns the received image to the original 
embedding coordinates. Without such a module, the system is vulner-
able to even mild geometric attacks (e.g., 10◦ rotation yields 11.3% 
FPR). We recommend using Fourier‑Mellin transform or feature‑based 
registration as a pre‑processing step when geometric transformations 
are expected. This limitation is not a minor caveat but a genuine security 
constraint.The manuscript reports two different false positive Figures: a 
theoretical target of 10⁻⁴ (0.01 percent) used for threshold selection in 
Section 3.2.3, and empirical test set rates of 0.1 percent to 0.7 percent 
under benign transformations. This discrepancy is not an inconsistency 
but a consequence of applying benign transformations. The theoretical 
target refers to the false positive rate on authentic watermarked images 
with no post-embedding processing. When a benign transformation (e. 
g., JPEG quality 70) is applied, the fragile watermark error δf increases, 
moving some authentic images closer to the decision boundary. The 
dual-watermark rule requires both δf > τf AND δsr > τsr to flag manip-
ulation. Under benign transformations, δf may exceed τf, but δsr remains 
low (because the semi-robust watermark survives), so the image is 
correctly classified as authentic. The observed false positives (0.1 
percent to 0.7 percent) occur only when both errors exceed their 
thresholds simultaneously, which is rare. Thus, the two Figures are 
complementary rather than contradictory. 4.8.5 Chaotic encryption for 
watermark key security
Our current pseudo-random watermark generator can be enhanced 
using higher-dimensional chaotic systems (Section 3.2). Three- 
dimensional Logistic maps, non-degenerate hyperchaotic systems, and 
sine-based reconstruction approaches offer larger key spaces, greater 
sensitivity to initial conditions, and improved randomness of chaotic 
sequences under finite-precision implementation. Integrating these 
chaotic systems would replace only the pseudo-random generator while 
leaving the embedding and detection equations unchanged. We consider 
this an orthogonal enhancement that can be incorporated without 
retraining the autoencoder or attention modules. Future work will 
compare these chaotic alternatives against the baseline in terms of key 
space size, correlation of generated watermarks, and resistance to brute- 
force attacks.
4.8.5. Robust feature learning for attention guidance
The feature learning approach proposed in "From simple to complex 
scenes: Learning robust feature representations for accurate human 
parsing" offers a promising direction for improving our multi-scale 
attention module. That method learns feature representations 
invariant to scene complexity, which could be adapted to medical im-
aging where image quality and pathology appearance vary significantly. 
A potential integration would replace fixed-kernel paths with learnable 
kernels whose receptive fields adapt to local feature complexity, guided 
by a robust feature loss. This could increase attention map precision for 
diffuse pathologies and improve watermark concentration without 
retraining the entire framework.
4.8.6. Embedding capacity and payload length trade-offs
PFF-WM supports a maximum fragile payload of 258 kbits and a 
semi-robust payload of 90 kbits for a 512 × 512 image (Section 3.2). 
However, increasing payload length beyond 64+32 kbits provides 
negligible accuracy gains (<0.2%) while increasing inference time by up 
to 23% (from 44 ms to 54 ms), as validated in Section 4.3. This trade-off 
arises because the stacked autoencoder processes a feature tensor X of 
size H x W x (N_f + N_sr), and the reconstruction error computation 
scales linearly with the payload dimension. For practical deployment, 
we recommend the default conFig. uration (N_f = 64 kbits, N_sr = 32 
kbits). If higher capacity is required, a lightweight transformer-based 
detector could replace the autoencoder to reduce the dimensionality 
bottleneck.
4.8.7. Uncertainty analysis and failure modes
The results reported in Tables 2–8 include standard deviations over 5 
runs (typically 0.6–1.8% for accuracy, 0.005–0.02 for AUC). Across the 
three datasets, detection accuracy varies by <2 percentage points 
(CheXpert: 97.6%, LiTS: 96.8%, ISIC 2019: 97.1%), indicating that PFF- 
WM generalises across modalities. However, two failure modes are 
notable. First, when the attention temperature τ in Eq. (4) is set too low 
(τ < 0.3) or too high (τ > 1.5), the attention map becomes either too 
sparse (concentrating on < 5% of pixels) or too diffuse (sparsity > 60%), 
both of which degrade localisation IoU by 5–10 points. The chosen

4.8.8. Future work directions
Future work will address the remaining shortcomings by moving 
towards self‑supervised explainability that no longer requires a dedi-
cated pretrained classifier, for instance by leveraging foundation models 
such as Med‑SAM to generate region‑of‑interest masks on the fly. This 
shift will naturally facilitate the extension of PFF‑WM to video streams 
like endoscopic or ultrasound sequences, where temporal consistency of 
the watermarks can be exploited for additional robustness. At the same 
time, the training pipeline will be enriched with an adaptive attack 
simulator based on generative adversarial learning, enabling the de-
tector to continuously adapt to novel manipulation strategies without 
manual intervention. Finally, the system will be deployed in a 
real‑world hospital pilot study to quantitatively assess its impact on 
radiologists' workflow efficiency and diagnostic confidence, which re-
mains the ultimate validation of any clinically oriented forensic tool.
5. Conclusion
This work introduced Proactive Forensic Fragile Watermarking (PFF- 
WM) for medical image authentication. The framework combines a 
fragile DWT watermark (pixel-level sensitivity) with an attention- 
guided semi-robust DCT watermark that prioritises diagnostically rele-
vant regions. Detection uses a stacked autoencoder trained on authentic 
images, and a Grad-CAM-based explainability layer estimates clinical 
impact. On CheXpert, LiTS, and ISIC 2019, PFF-WM achieves 97.6% 
detection accuracy, 83.2% localisation IoU, and a false positive rate 
below 0.5% under benign transformations, with 44 ms inference per 512 
× 512 image. The main limitations are dependency on a pretrained 
classifier for explainability (unavailable for rare pathologies), reduced 
effectiveness for diffuse pathologies, and sensitivity to unseen attack 
types and geometric transformations. Future work will eliminate the 
dependency on a pretrained classifier by adopting self‑supervised 
explainability grounded in foundation models such as Med‑SAM, which 
will naturally enable extension to video streams (endoscopic or 
ultrasound sequences). Concurrently, the training pipeline will incor-
porate adaptive attack simulation through generative adversarial 
learning to improve robustness against unseen manipulation strategies. 
Finally, the system will be evaluated in hospital environments to mea-
sure its effect on radiologists' workflow efficiency and diagnostic 
confidence.
